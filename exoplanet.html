Exploring Exoplanets: Analysing 501 TESS Stars Using Lightkurve and Archival Data

By Dharanesh Palaniappan | Research in Observational Astronomy | 2026

Hello friends,

The discovery of planets beyond our Solar System has transformed our understanding of planetary systems and their diversity. With thousands of confirmed exoplanets now available in astronomical archives, studying their properties and the characteristics of their host stars provides an opportunity to investigate how planetary systems form, evolve and differ from one another.

In this project, I worked with data from the Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (TESS), using the Python package Lightkurve to examine stellar light curves and search for transit-like signals. I also compared the selected stars with existing astronomical databases to identify known exoplanets, TESS Objects of Interest (TOIs), and variable stars.

Alongside the photometric analysis, I compiled planetary and orbital parameters and calculated additional physical quantities to investigate the properties of the selected planetary systems.

1. Introduction

The discovery of the first extrasolar planets around a pulsar system in 1992 (Wolszczan & Frail, 1992), followed by the discovery of 51 Pegasi b around a Sun-like star in 1995 (Mayor & Queloz, 1995), marked important milestones in modern astronomy.

Among the different techniques used to detect exoplanets, the transit method is one of the most widely used. When a planet passes in front of its host star, as observed from Earth, a small reduction in the measured stellar brightness occurs. This reduction appears as a dip in a light curve.

The transit depth, orbital period and duration of the signal can provide information about the planetary system. Space missions such as Kepler and TESS have made it possible to observe large numbers of stars and identify potential planetary systems through high-precision photometry.

The objective of this project was to examine a selected sample of TESS stars, analyse their photometric light curves, compare the results with established exoplanet catalogues and investigate the physical properties of the associated planetary systems.

2. Data and Sample Selection

For this study, I used the TESS Candidate Target List (CTL) version 8.01, obtained from the Mikulski Archive for Space Telescopes (MAST). The catalogue contains stellar information for a large number of targets observed or selected for TESS observations.

From this catalogue, approximately 500–501 stars were selected using a set of stellar parameters. The selection was intended to focus on a relatively restricted range of stellar properties for the subsequent analysis.

3. Methodology

The analysis involved three main stages: photometric light-curve analysis, cross-matching with astronomical databases and examination of the planetary parameters.

3.1 Lightkurve analysis

The light curves of the selected stars were retrieved from MAST using the Lightkurve Python package. The downloaded light curves were processed to remove invalid values and outliers before the periodicity analysis.

The Box Least Squares (BLS) method was used to search for periodic transit-like signals. BLS is particularly useful for identifying periodic dips in stellar brightness that may be associated with planetary transits.

The Lomb–Scargle periodogram was also used to investigate periodic variations in the light curves. This method is useful for examining periodic signals, including stellar variability and rotation-related modulation.

3.2 Cross-matching with astronomical databases

To compare the photometric analysis with previously available astronomical information, the selected targets were cross-matched with three databases.

  • NASA Exoplanet Archive: Used to identify previously confirmed exoplanets and retrieve their published planetary and orbital parameters.
  • exo.MAST: Used to examine TESS Objects of Interest (TOIs) and associated candidate information.
  • SIMBAD: Used to investigate stellar classifications and variability information.

This cross-matching process helped distinguish previously confirmed planets from objects listed as candidates and stars with known variability classifications.

3.3 Planetary parameter analysis

For the identified planetary systems, I compiled parameters such as planetary radius, orbital period, transit duration, impact parameter, equilibrium temperature, transit depth, semi-major axis, planetary mass, eccentricity and orbital inclination.

Additional physical quantities were also calculated or compiled, including planetary density, escape velocity, insolation, surface gravity, orbital velocity, Safronov number, geometric transit probability and Hill radius. These parameters provide a broader view of the physical and dynamical properties of the selected planetary systems.

5. Understanding the Planetary Parameters

The compiled parameters provide several ways to examine the physical characteristics of the planetary systems. Some of the important quantities considered in this project are described below.

  • Planetary radius: Provides an estimate of the planet's size relative to Jupiter.
  • Orbital period: Describes the time taken by a planet to complete one revolution around its host star.
  • Equilibrium temperature: Provides an estimate of the planet's temperature under an assumed energy-balance model.
  • Planetary density: Helps investigate the possible bulk composition of a planet when reliable mass and radius measurements are available.
  • Escape velocity: Describes the minimum speed required to escape the gravitational field of the planet.
  • Insolation: Represents the stellar energy received by a planet relative to a specified reference.
  • Surface gravity: Describes the gravitational acceleration at the planetary surface.
  • Orbital velocity: Describes the planet's orbital speed under the adopted orbital model.
  • Safronov number: Provides a dimensionless measure related to a planet's gravitational scattering and accretion capabilities.
  • Geometric transit probability: Estimates the likelihood that an orbit is aligned to produce a transit from our line of sight.
  • Hill radius: Estimates the region around a planet in which its gravitational influence dominates over that of the host star.

6. Broader Exoplanet Catalogue Analysis

In addition to examining the selected TESS sample, the NASA Exoplanet Archive was used to obtain a broader collection of confirmed exoplanet data. The larger catalogue provides an opportunity to investigate relationships between planetary properties and host-star characteristics beyond the initial sample.

The broader analysis can be used to explore distributions and possible correlations involving planetary radius, mass, orbital period, stellar temperature, stellar mass and other host-star parameters.

These relationships must be interpreted with care. The observed population of exoplanets is influenced by detection biases, observational selection effects and the sensitivity of different detection methods. Consequently, the observed distribution does not necessarily represent the intrinsic population of planetary systems.

7. Discussion

The combination of TESS photometric analysis and archival cross-matching provides a useful approach for examining known planetary systems and identifying objects that warrant further investigation. Lightkurve makes it possible to process and inspect photometric time-series data, while the NASA Exoplanet Archive, exo.MAST and SIMBAD provide complementary information about planets, candidates and stellar classifications.

The results also demonstrate why transit-like signals should not be interpreted in isolation. Stellar variability, eclipsing binaries, instrumental effects and other astrophysical phenomena can produce brightness variations that resemble planetary transits. Comparing photometric results with established catalogues and examining the properties of the host stars are therefore important steps in the analysis.

The planetary parameter dataset offers an additional way to investigate the diversity of the selected systems. However, comparisons between different detection methods should account for differences in measurement precision, parameter availability and the assumptions used to derive physical quantities.

8. Data Sources and Parameter Disclaimer

The stellar, planetary and orbital parameters presented in this project were obtained, compiled or derived using astronomical data and published information available through the following databases:

  • NASA Exoplanet Archive
  • exo.MAST
  • SIMBAD Astronomical Database

The NASA Exoplanet Archive was used to identify previously confirmed exoplanets and retrieve their published planetary and orbital parameters. exo.MAST was used to examine TESS Objects of Interest and associated candidate information. SIMBAD was used to investigate stellar classifications and variability information.

Additional physical parameters calculated in this project were derived using the available database values and the corresponding physical relations. These calculated quantities should therefore be understood as derived results based on archival input parameters, rather than independent observational measurements.

The Lightkurve analysis was performed separately to examine TESS photometric light curves and transit-like signals. The recovery or reanalysis of a photometric signal associated with a previously known planet is not, by itself, an independent confirmation or discovery of that planet. Confirmed planets, TOI candidates and other variable objects are treated as distinct categories in this study.

Any differences between the values presented here and those available in the original databases may arise from adopted input parameters, reference values, calculation assumptions, rounding or subsequent updates to the archival catalogues. The original databases remain the primary sources for the underlying published measurements and classifications.

9. Summary and Conclusions

This project presents an analysis of a selected sample of approximately 500–501 stars from the TESS Candidate Target List v8.01. Their photometric light curves were examined using Lightkurve, with BLS and Lomb–Scargle methods used to investigate transit-like and periodic signals.

Cross-matching with the NASA Exoplanet Archive, exo.MAST and SIMBAD provided information about confirmed exoplanets, TOI candidates and stellar variability. The original manuscript reports 30 confirmed exoplanets, 44 TOI candidates and 59 variable stars in the selected sample.

The project also involved compiling planetary and orbital parameters and examining additional physical quantities, including density, escape velocity, insolation, surface gravity, orbital velocity, Safronov number, transit probability and Hill radius.

Overall, this work combines photometric time-series analysis with archival astronomical data to examine exoplanetary systems and their host stars. It provides a foundation for further investigation of planetary properties, stellar characteristics and the observational biases that influence the currently known exoplanet population.
